About SkySift

SkySift searches archival Kepler light curves for small dips in brightness that repeat. Every repeating dip goes through a ladder of checks, and a person decides what it is.

How SkySift works

A light curve is a star's brightness measured again and again over time. Kepler watched one patch of sky for four years. SkySift searches those light curves, starting with the faint background stars Kepler's own pipeline never searched. A planet that crosses its star on every orbit dims it a little, at the same interval each time.

Before the search, SkySift removes the star's own regular brightness waves, such as pulsation and spots turning with the star. It then tries every period from {{ "%g" | format(window[0]) }} to {{ "%g" | format(window[1]) }} days and scores how well a short, flat-bottomed dip repeats at each. The strongest period becomes a signal when its score clears the detection threshold.

Each signal then climbs a ladder of checks, cheapest first. A check passes, fails or cannot reach a verdict, and the ladder stops at the first failure. The rungs, in order:

The detection threshold is measured, not guessed. SkySift runs the same search on light curves that cannot hold a real transit: each one flipped upside down, and each one shuffled within its quarters. The threshold sits above the strongest score that noise alone produced, with a margin. It is measured again after every change to the search.

A signal that cannot get a verdict is not thrown away. At the end of every sweep, SkySift takes each undetermined signal and each stage 1 candidate and tries the step that can settle it. The steps are listed below.

The engine never approves anything. A person opens each candidate, reads why it passed, looks at the plots and decides: approve, reject or ask for more data. Every decision is kept with its note. A later decision adds to the record and erases nothing.
