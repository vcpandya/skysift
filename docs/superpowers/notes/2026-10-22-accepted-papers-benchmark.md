# Benchmark of the two SkySift notes against accepted candidate papers

Prepared 22 October 2026 for the referee pack of KIC 6146676 ("A 40.291 day transit candidate on KIC 6146676") and KIC 5689121 ("An independent recovery of the 13.769 day transit candidate on KIC 5689121"). I read both `note.tex` files and both pack READMEs first, then the literature, then the pack data behind a few numbers (`data/rung_metrics.json`, `data/cross_check.json`, `provenance/calibration.json`, `data/stellar_provenance.csv`).

## 0. Scope, method and two caveats

- **RNAAS is moderated, not refereed.** The AAS Research Notes page says the journal is "moderated but not edited". Notes there are accepted by the lead editor for fit, not by a referee. So the RNAAS examples below show what gets *published* as a candidate note. The refereed vetting standard comes from the catalogue and validation papers in section 2. The "referee" questions in section 5 apply to the moderator, to readers who later cite or follow up the note, and to the refereed paper that the README needs for a community TOI and for the NASA Exoplanet Archive.
- **How I found the notes.** I queried Crossref for the RNAAS ISSN (2515-5172) with transit and candidate terms, ran the arXiv API on journal-ref and comment fields, and used web search. I read nine notes in full. Three (Gollotti 2024, Kaplan 2026, Mason 2026) are known only from their Crossref abstracts, because the IOP site answered with a bot-check redirect. I did not try to get around it.
- RNAAS limits (AAS page, read today): 1,500 words or fewer, an abstract is required, and one figure or one table, not both. Both drafts fit: 1,394 and 1,473 words. KIC 5689121 has 27 words left, so anything added there needs a matching cut.

## 4. The common pattern of an accepted candidate note (one page)

1. **Title.** It names the object and the status ("candidate", "probably", or a question mark). None claims a planet. The two that overclaim (R11 "validates", R8's implausible a/R*) were accepted anyway, but they are not models to copy.
2. **Abstract (required since 2020).** Data set, period, depth, duration, radius, and one sentence on status.
3. **Signal paragraph.** Mission, sectors or quarters, light-curve product, detrending, search algorithm, and the detection statistic against a stated threshold (S/N 10.1 against 7.1 in R1, SDE 54.2 against 9 in R9, SDE 47.9 against 7 in R6). Notes that use an ephemeris give BJD_TDB, and the better ones fit a line to individual mid-times with parenthetical 1-sigma errors (R9).
4. **Vetting paragraph.** Odd and even depths, a secondary eclipse or its upper limit, and a pixel-level test (centroid, difference image, per-pixel light curves or varied apertures). TESS-era notes cite LATTE or DAVE and are mostly qualitative. Only R3 (secondary < 303 ppm, 3 sigma) and R11 (0.003 px) give numbers. The best Kepler-era note (R1) compares the stellar density from each transit with the host's to argue which star is transited.
5. **False-positive status.** Most notes give no FPP at all. Notes that are careful stop at "candidate" and name the follow-up that would settle it (RV in R3 and R10, photometry of the neighbour in R1). Refereed independent-search papers keep candidates as candidates when S/N is low or follow-up is missing, even with FPP < 1% (Kunimoto 2020, citing Burke 2019).
6. **Stellar parameters.** A catalogue source (TIC, EPIC, Berger 2018, Gaia) is named in one sentence. Few notes derive them, and almost none discuss reddening or binarity.
7. **One figure, often multi-panel.** A fold with the model is universal. Most TESS notes add a periodogram, odd and even folds, or a centroid or difference image in the same figure (R5, R6, R8, R9, R10).
8. **Follow-up and TESS.** Kepler and K2 notes from 2019 on usually say whether TESS sees the target (R6 used TESS data, and R12 spans TESS years). Few give a predicted transit window.
9. **Prior work and priority.** Catalogues already listing the object are checked (ExoFOP in R4, KOI and TOI lists elsewhere), and overlaps are credited explicitly, even late (R2, Cabrera 2014, Schmitt 2014).
10. **Data and code.** Usually thin: a MAST DOI (R9, R12), a figure tarball and GitHub (R3), or nothing. A frozen Zenodo record of the vetting products, as SkySift plans, is above the RNAAS norm.

In short, an accepted note is a claim of a periodic signal plus the minimum vetting needed to make it worth following up, written as a candidate, with a citable ephemeris. The SkySift notes already exceed the RNAAS norm on ephemeris errors, stellar parameters, null-search calibration, TRICERATOPS and data provenance. Their exposure is with a referee and with expert readers. Such a reader checks the numbers against each other and against the Kepler-era standard in section 2.
