Transit candidate · Kepler field · never a Kepler target

KIC 6146676

Gaia DR3 2073896294004902272 RA 19h 51m 28.49s Dec +41° 27′ 20.0″ G 16.24 BP−RP 2.10 M1 dwarf · 303 pc
Candidate · all tests passed

A transit-like dip repeats every 40.3 days on a faint red dwarf that the Kepler mission never selected as a target. The signal passes every SkySift vetting test, the pixel data place it on this star and not on its bright neighbour, and no planet or binary catalogue lists it. If real, it is a planet of about 2.6 Earth radii on a temperate 40-day orbit around an M dwarf 303 parsecs away. It stays a candidate until independent follow-up confirms it.

The signal

Every 40.29 days the star dims by about 0.22 percent for just under four hours. Folding four years of Kepler photometry on that period stacks 31 individual dips into one clean transit.

02

Does it repeat?

A real transit comes back on schedule with the same depth. Of the 37 transits predicted across the mission, 33 fell on nights Kepler was observing, and each one shows the dip. The depth does not drift with time, season or spacecraft orientation.

03

The checks a planet has to pass

Most periodic dips are not planets. They are eclipsing binary stars, blended neighbours, or instrument artefacts. SkySift runs the same families of tests the Kepler team used, and this signal clears all of them.

Odd versus even transits expert

If the true period were 80.6 d and we were seeing two different stars eclipse each other, alternate dips would differ in depth. They do not.

Phase 0.5: the secondary eclipse test expert

A stellar companion would show its own eclipse half an orbit later. The window at phase 0.5 is flat; the primary is shown at the same scale.

Depth by Kepler season expert

The spacecraft rolled 90° every quarter, so each season places the star on a different detector. A blend or an artefact usually changes depth with season. Here χ² = 0.55 for 3 degrees of freedom, p = 0.91.
04

Which star is dimming?

KIC 6146676 sits 11.5″ (2.9 Kepler pixels) from KIC 6146666, a star 23 times brighter that Kepler was actually observing. Subtracting in-transit from out-of-transit pixel images shows where the missing light comes from. In all four seasons it comes from the faint star.

05

The star, and what the planet would be

Gaia's colour and parallax make the host a cool dwarf. The transit depth gives the planet-to-star radius ratio directly; the planet's size, orbit and temperature follow from the stellar parameters and carry their uncertainties.

How to reproduce this

Everything here comes from public archives. The light curve is the KBonus background-star product on MAST; the pixel data are Kepler's own target pixel files for KIC 6146666; the catalogue checks are logged with URL, query and checksum.

SKYSIFT_THRESHOLDS__SDE_MIN=13 python -m skysift vet --source kbonus_bkg --target-id kic-006146676 --spectrum
